    Where the orange is

    Land scoring 50 or more, by country. The bar is the share of that country.

      How places are marked

      What turns a place orange

      Two things. It is far from every way in, and the ground is somewhere people could not live.

      A way in is a road, track, railway, town, airstrip, research station, mine, archaeological site, river or coast. The map measures how far each patch of land, about 10 km across, is from the nearest one. Around 100 km off counts as far. Past 300 km it hardly gets more remote.

      What holds a score down

      Ground people have used for a long time. Ice can reach 100, because nobody ever lived on it. Open desert and very high country stop near 75. Forest, grassland and tundra stop near 55, because people have lived on or crossed that ground for thousands of years. Lights at night pull a score down further.

      Why each region looks the way it does

      • Antarctica is almost solid orange. It is ice, it never had a native population, and the stations are small dots on a continent.
      • Greenland is orange inland. People live on the coast and the middle is ice sheet.
      • The Sahara and Arabia are patchy. The big dune seas are far from any track, but nomads crossed them, so they are capped.
      • Tibet has some orange. It is high and far from roads, but herders use it.
      • Siberia and Arctic Canada show orange only on remote mountains and islands. Hunters ranged widely everywhere else.
      • The Amazon is barely marked. Rivers reach nearly all of it and people have lived along them for thousands of years.
      • Australia is mostly unmarked. Tracks cross the deserts and people have lived there for tens of thousands of years.

      The evidence check

      The score is a guess from distance. When you tap a spot, the page also asks four public sources what is on record within 10 km: photos, wildlife records, iNaturalist sightings and Wikipedia articles. If any come back, somebody was there, whatever the score says.

      Where it is wrong

      Nobody keeps a record of everywhere people have stood. The map only knows the camps, mines and expedition routes that are in its data, and some are missing. Hunters, herders and nomads went far beyond any road. The oceans are not scored.
