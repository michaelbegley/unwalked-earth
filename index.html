            How it works

            What turns a place orange

            It is far from any way in, and nobody could live on the ground.

            A way in is a road, track, railway, town, airstrip, research station, mine, archaeological site, river or coast. In Antarctica any recorded visit counts too. The map measures how far each patch of land, about 10 km across, is from the nearest one. 100 km counts as far. Past 300 km it stops mattering.

            What holds a score down

            Ground people have used for a long time. Ice can reach 100, because nobody ever lived on it. Open desert and very high country stop near 75. Forest, grassland and tundra stop near 55, because people have lived on or crossed that ground for thousands of years. Lights at night pull a score down further.

            Regions

              Records

              The score is a guess from distance. When you tap a spot, the page also asks four public sources what is on record within 10 km: photos, wildlife records, iNaturalist sightings and Wikipedia articles. If anything comes back, somebody was there.

              Time

              The slider goes back 200,000 years. Land our species had not reached yet is orange. Dates come from the earliest known site or settlement in each region and are argued over. Africa counts as walked from the start. Inside a region people had reached, the slider shows today's orange, so the past looks more walked than it was.

              Where it is wrong

              Nobody keeps a record of everywhere people have stood. The map only knows the camps, mines and expedition routes that are in its data, and some are missing. Hunters, herders and nomads went far beyond any road. The oceans are not scored.

              Sources

              • Natural Earth: land, roads, rivers, ice
              • Malaria Atlas Project: travel time to cities
              • OurAirports: airstrips, open and closed
              • COMNAP: Antarctic stations
              • Wikipedia, first human settlements: dates for the time slider
              • Leihy and others, 2020: recorded visits in Antarctica, CC BY-NC 4.0
              • Wikidata: mines and archaeological sites
              • GeoNames: towns of 1,000 or more
              • NASA: night lights
              • On tap: Sentinel-2 cloudless by EOX and NASA Blue Marble pictures, Wikipedia, Wikimedia Commons, GBIF, iNaturalist, Open-Meteo, OpenStreetMap search

              Privacy

              No cookies. No accounts. Saved places stay on your device.

              Each visit adds one to a count, and one to the kind of site it came from, like Reddit or Google. Nothing about you is kept.

              Feedback is stored as the text you send. No name or address goes with it.

              When you tap a place, your browser asks Wikipedia, Wikimedia Commons, GBIF, iNaturalist, Open-Meteo, EOX and NASA about that spot. Search asks OpenStreetMap.
